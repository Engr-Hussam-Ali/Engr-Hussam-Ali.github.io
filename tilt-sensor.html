Why a tilt sensor reads a tilt that isn't there

Thermal-structural finite-element analysis and layout optimisation of an IoT tilt-sensor package, for the Safety IoT project of KRISS, South Korea's national measurement standards laboratory, carried out at Sejong University.

In short

  • The false tilt is a temperature effect: the thermal UR1 is 229 to 257 times the self-weight UR1 in every configuration.
  • How the box is fixed decides one tilt axis (−1605 to +558 µrad) but hardly touches the other (spread of 47 µrad).
  • On a wall, a stiff epoxy bead along the long edges gives the smallest error; a soft silicone bead leaves about 22 times more.
  • Moving three PCB screws and four housing stems cut the thermal error by 97.8 % in the optimisation model.

The problem

Low-cost MEMS tilt sensors are used to watch ageing buildings for movement. In a 2022–2023 field test on a retaining wall in Seoul, their output drifted over months and within each day, and followed the air temperature: the correlation reached 0.96 for one sensor. That drift is larger than the structural tilt the sensors are meant to detect.

The output can be written as the true tilt plus two noise terms: noise from the MEMS chip itself, and noise from the package around it. The chip is a bought part, but the package is designed, so the package term is the one this work sets out to understand and reduce.

The package and the model

The chip sits on a small board, which sits on spacers on the main board, which is held by three screws to an inner housing. That inner housing hangs from the top lid on four stems. Anything in this chain that bends or rotates is read by the chip as tilt.

The output is the rotation of the chip's centre node about two axes, UR1 and UR2, in microradians (1 µrad is a tilt of 1 mm over 1 km). The sensor can be bonded to a wall or to a floor, so both installations were modelled.

Five phases

First prove the model, then use it. Phase 2 optimises the internal layout as a separate study; phases 3 to 5 study the fixing, the temperature load and the installation angle on the original layout.

Phase 1Model validation

No measurements of the package response existed, so the model had to be shown to be numerically sound before it could guide any decision. The model inherited from the previous year produced singularities, tie reversions and negative eigenvalues. Three fixes removed all of them:

  • Ties changed from node-to-surface to surface-to-surface, which averages the tie over the contact patch instead of pulling single nodes, and removes stress spikes.
  • The PCB surface was partitioned around each screw, so the same faces are tied at every mesh size and convergence becomes monotonic.
  • The main PCB carried a hidden 0.1° tilt: its normal was (−1.745×10−3, 0, 0.999998) instead of (0, 0, 1). That pushed the contact surfaces outside the tie tolerance and caused five tie reversions and nine singularities. Rotating it back removed every one.

Mesh convergence

The element size at the chip was halved three times, from 1.0 to 0.125 mm. The 0.25 mm mesh was adopted: refining it further changes UR1 by 0.15 % and UR2 by 0.07 %.

Eleven independent checks

Each check asks whether a finer mesh elsewhere, another element or tie setting, or another way of reading the results would change the answer. All passed, and the last one showed that the adhesive's temperature-dependent stiffness has to be modelled.

Phase 2Layout optimisation

Three screws hold the main PCB and four stems carry the inner housing. These seven parts sit directly in the load path and can be moved without redesigning the package. The aim: find the positions that minimise the chip rotation under a 50 °C temperature rise.

  • Seven design variables: one coordinate per screw and per stem, bounded by the board edges, part radii and tolerances.
  • Objective: minimise RMS = √(UR1² + UR2²) at the chip.
  • Constraints: at least the screw radius plus 1 mm from the board edge, at least 5 mm between a stem and a screw, and the lower stems may not cross the board centreline.

Each finite-element run is expensive, so the search used surrogates: 80 Latin-hypercube samples, an automated Abaqus batch that moves the parts, solves and extracts the rotations, then two Kriging models built with the DACE toolbox. UR1 and UR2 each get their own surrogate, because the RMS has a sharp minimum where both outputs cross zero, which a Gaussian process cannot fit well. Adaptive infill near the predicted optimum grew the training set from 80 to 102 points, and the optimum was confirmed by a final analysis: 103 runs in all.

Phase 3Fixing conditions

In the field the box is glued or screwed to a wall or a floor, and the fixing decides how the housing is held as it expands. Nineteen fixings were compared under identical loads:

  • 11 on a wall: a stiff epoxy (Araldite 2015) or a soft silicone (Sylgard 184) in four bond patterns, plus three screw layouts;
  • 8 on a floor: the same two adhesives and four patterns.

Each was loaded by self-weight in nine orientations at −10 and +40 °C and by a uniform 50 °C rise: 216 load cases. The self-weight cases are linear perturbation steps, so the combined cases are sums, checked against direct solves (check 7).

  • On a wall, the epoxy along the full surface, the full perimeter or the long edges gives the smallest thermal UR2 (−56 to −280 µrad); the long-edge bond ranks first.
  • The silicone gives −1223 to −1300 µrad whatever the pattern: about 22 times the epoxy long-edge bond.
  • The one asymmetric screw layout reverses the sign (+558 µrad). That looks good, but it comes from over-cancellation, explained in phase 4.

Phase 4Thermal mechanism

Inside an ageing building the temperature can range from about −10 to +40 °C. The ABS housing and spacers expand 3.5 times as much as the FR-4 boards, so the package bends.

The temperature load dominates: its UR1 is 229 to 257 times the self-weight UR1 in all 19 fixings. Thermal UR1 hardly depends on the fixing (spread 47 µrad), while thermal UR2 ranges from −1605 to +558 µrad. So the fixing governs the error, through one axis.

To find out why, I decomposed the motion of each part into a rigid rotation. The chip's thermal UR2 is the rotation of the inner housing plus an almost constant contribution from the board stack (−1416 to −1543 µrad). The housing hangs from the top lid, so a fixing that restrains the lid makes the inner housing turn the other way and cancel most of the board-stack rotation.

  • Wall, epoxy (full surface, perimeter or long edges): the stiff bead holds the lid, the inner housing turns by +1165 to +1371 µrad and cancels most of the board-stack rotation. Bonding only the short edges holds the lid less and cancels about half.
  • Wall, silicone: the soft bead barely holds the lid, so about 80 % of the board-stack rotation remains.
  • Floor, epoxy (full surface, perimeter or long edges): nothing holds the lid, and the board-stack rotation remains almost in full (−1466 to −1605 µrad).
  • Asymmetric screws: a large lid rotation over-cancels the board stack, which explains the positive sign.

The smallest output (−56 µrad) is the residual of +1371 and −1427 µrad. So the ranking of fixings is reliable, but small absolute values are sensitive to every modelling assumption. A constant adhesive stiffness alone would change the epoxy result by 22 %.

Phase 5Installation orientation

A sensor is never mounted exactly level. Rotating gravity by up to ±10° about either axis changed the output RMS by at most 0.91 %, and by less than 0.2 % in 16 of 19 fixings. The effect follows the gravity vector, not the angle: the sin θ part acts linearly and the 1 − cos θ part quadratically.

Wall or floor matters through the fixing. With epoxy, the wall gives a far smaller thermal error than the floor (−56 to −280 against −1466 to −1605 µrad), because only on the wall does the bead hold the lid. The best combined result is 1568 µrad on a wall (epoxy, long edges) and 1710 µrad on a floor.

What the study shows

  • The package noise is essentially a temperature effect; self-weight and installation angle are second-order.
  • Temperature and self-weight responses can be added, because the model is linear; superposition matches direct solves to 4.3×10−8.
  • The fixing sets UR2 through the cancellation between the inner housing and the board stack, but cannot change UR1.
  • For wall installation, an epoxy bead along the long edges is the best of the 19 fixings studied.
  • Re-positioning the internal screws and stems is the most effective single measure: 97.8 % less thermal error in the optimisation model.

Limits and next steps

The wall and floor are rigid and do not expand, there is no contact between the inner housing and the enclosure, and the results compare fixings rather than predict absolute readings. Accurate absolute values need a higher-fidelity model that relaxes these assumptions; once the fixing is chosen, the layout optimisation should be repeated on that model.
